When planning a day, we usually make a timetable. A at 10, B at 1, C in the evening.  
It looks airtight. Yet real days always unravel differently. An unexpected message, a meeting that overruns, a postponed reply slip in one after another, and the mind keeps changing modes: writing to responding, responding to deciding, deciding back to execution.

The problem is not too much work. It is **too much switching**.

In CH10, we built a firewall against decision fatigue. CH11 is the next step. Another resource drains away even before decisions: context. The cost of holding in mind what I was doing, why I was doing it and what comes next. When that cost grows, action stops even with time remaining.

This chapter’s core is therefore simple: manage the **Context Budget** before managing time. Limit daily switches, consciously create boundaries and leave reentry sentences to reduce lost flow. Instead of squeezing out more willpower, lose less context.

## <span className="text-emerald-400 font-bold mb-6 flex items-center gap-3">Context Budget: Set the Number of Daily Switches First</span>

The difference between weak and strong focus lies more in design than talent. Strong performers do not succeed because they never become distracted; they block likely distractions in advance. They particularly limit switches.

We usually record how many hours we worked today. But how many times we changed modes often explains actual results better. From writing to checking messages, from replying to the calendar, from the calendar to browsing, then back to the document. This creates a cost greater than the task’s difficulty itself: **the cost of realignment**.

CH11 recommends dividing the day into three blocks.

- **Deep Block:** A period requiring demanding output. No more than 1 switch.
- **Admin Block:** Messages, scheduling and lightweight processing. Allow 3–5 switches.
- **Reset Block:** Organization, logs and fixing the next starting point. No more than 2 switches.

The key is not perfect allocation of time. It is explicitly stating the permitted switches in each block. Exceeding the limit is treated as a deviation from design rather than a lack of ability, making correction possible instead of self-blame.

A context budget is not a rule that removes freedom. It is insurance protecting the focus important work requires. Decide what to switch less before considering what to do more, and the density of a day changes.

## <span className="text-cyan-400 font-bold mb-6 mt-16 flex items-center gap-3">Boundary Rituals: Fix Starts and Endings with Brief Rituals</span>

The more frequently we switch, the longer starts become and the vaguer endings grow. After thinking “I should begin,” we open and close apps three times; after finishing, we leave no next step and only accumulate browser tabs. Closing this gap requires ritual, not willpower: **short, repeatable boundary rituals**, rather than elaborate routines.

The recommended basic structure takes only 90 seconds.

**Starting ritual: 45 seconds**  
1) Pause notifications  
2) Read today’s one-line goal aloud  
3) Click the first-action button (open the document, start the timer)

**Closing ritual: 45 seconds**  
1) Record where you stopped in one line  
2) Record the next first action in one line  
3) Close windows, leaving only 3 relevant ones

Many routines fail because they are too long. On tired days, even good habits become burdens. A 45-second ritual can still happen in a poor state. Repeatability matters more than sophistication.

As these rituals accumulate, the brain quickly recognizes “focus mode begins now” and “this is closing mode.” Less ambiguity lowers switching costs. Flow is maintained by repeated boundaries rather than enthusiasm.

## <span className="text-blue-400 font-bold mb-6 mt-16 flex items-center gap-3">The Entry Line: Design the 30 Seconds After a Pause</span>

Major failures do not usually undo a day. Small interruptions do: a phone call, checking messages, one unexpected request. The problem is the delay in returning, not the interruption. Even 5 minutes of wandering afterward quickly cools the rhythm.

CH11’s most practical tool is therefore the **Entry Line**. The rule is simple: whenever you stop, leave the sentence that starts the next session. Make it an action rather than an abstraction.

- Bad sentence: “Continue tomorrow”  
- Good sentence: “Add 2 examples to the first paragraph under subheading 3, then write the concluding sentence”

A good entry line meets three conditions.

1. **Starts with a verb.** (Write, organize, compare, delete)
2. **Has a small scope.** (A unit finishable within 20 minutes)
3. **Has a completion criterion.** (How many lines, how many items, which state)

This one sentence dramatically reduces decisions at the moment of return. Instead of deciding what to do, execute the action already chosen. We often seek grand strategies, yet these tiny connecting devices are what actually save a day.

Extend entry lines to a team and they become stronger. Share the next first action in collaboration channels rather than leaving it only in private logs, and handoff quality improves. Personal productivity and team operations rest on the same principle: protecting continuity of context.

## <span className="text-yellow-400 font-bold mb-6 mt-16 flex items-center gap-3">Apply It Today: Set a Context Budget in 12 Minutes</span>

Try it today. A 12-minute timer is enough.

1. Roughly divide tomorrow into Deep/Admin/Reset blocks.  
2. Write the permitted switches for each block.  
3. Fix one sentence each for the starting and closing rituals.  
4. Leave one entry line for today’s last task.

At the end of the day, check only this: “Did I stay within my switching limit?” rather than “Did I stay on schedule?”

Changing this question changes how we see the day. We always want more time, but often need fewer switches. Focus is the result of designing to reduce losses, not a state of tension.

CH11’s conclusion is clear.  
Flow is a budget, not luck.  
And a budget is protected by rules, not willpower.

Tomorrow will bring variables and interruptions too. That cannot be avoided.  
But we can design a shorter interval from interruption to return.  
What we must protect is the ability to reconnect, not a perfect day.
