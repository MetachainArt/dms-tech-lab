<div className="lead text-2xl text-white font-sans font-medium mb-12 leading-relaxed">
What undermines productivity is not a colossal failure, but small, frequent inputs.
</div>

We begin each day with a plan: what to make, what to finish, and in what order.  
But in practice, our days fall apart more from **too much input** than too little planning. Work notifications, group chat messages, links we click without thinking, news we meant only to check, comments that hold us longer than expected. Each looks small and insignificant, but together they slowly twist the axis of the day.

In CH11, we explored managing the number of context switches as a budget. CH12 takes us one step earlier.  
Designing a way to reduce incoming inputs before a switch even happens. In other words, **Input Hygiene**.

The core of input hygiene is simple.  
Decide what to let in less of before deciding what more to take in.

![An abstract graphic of a workflow dashboard and attention budget meter, with no people](/blog/today_me_ch12_01.jpg)

## <span className="text-emerald-400 font-bold mb-6 flex items-center gap-3">Input Hygiene, Step 1: Separate Purposes, Not Channels</span>

Most people organize by app: messaging with messaging, email with email, documents with documents.  
But the brain does not tire because of app names. It tires **when purposes get mixed together**.

For example, three things can arrive simultaneously in the same messenger.

- An operational issue requiring immediate action
- A collaboration question that only needs an answer by the end of today
- Casual conversation you do not need to know about right now

The problem is that all three arrive at the same time, through the same notification, on the same screen.  
The moment we read the content, we begin making judgments; the moment we begin judging, our attention scatters.

So the first rule of CH12 is **labeling by purpose**, rather than “separating channels.”

- **Immediate**: Requires action within 10 minutes
- **Today**: Handle before the day’s deadline
- **Queue**: Group together and process in batches

What matters is not applying these labels perfectly.  
It is reclaiming the right to classify inputs yourself, so reading something does not automatically commandeer your judgment.  
Input hygiene is not an information management technique; it is an act of reclaiming sovereignty over your attention.

## <span className="text-cyan-400 font-bold mb-6 mt-16 flex items-center gap-3">Input Hygiene, Step 2: Fix Your Check-in Schedule and Limit Exceptions</span>

“Checking constantly” can look like responsibility, but often it is a habit for soothing anxiety.  
We imagine more checking will bring reassurance, but the more often we check, the more context we lose.

The recommended baseline schedule is simple.

- Messenger: 3 times a day (morning / afternoon / before finishing work)
- Email: 2 times a day (before lunch / before the end of work)
- Communities and news: Once a day (after work or during a reset block)

The point of this schedule is less about “how often you look” than clearly defining **when you do not look**.  
Closing an app during a focus block is not irresponsible; it is a commitment to protecting the quality of your core work.

Exceptions are necessary. But exceptions need conditions.

- Issues with a substantial service impact, such as customer outages, payment errors, or failed deployments
- Requests with an explicit deadline today
- Emergency contact rules agreed upon in advance

Leave other inputs under the principle: “Even if it is a little uncomfortable, I will review them together later.”  
Prioritize the concentrated effort of finishing over the pleasure of reacting immediately.

## <span className="text-blue-400 font-bold mb-6 mt-16 flex items-center gap-3">Input Hygiene, Step 3: Move It to a Queue Instead of Answering as Soon as You Read It</span>

When an input arrives, we instinctively choose one of two responses.  
Answer immediately, or feel guilty.  
But between these two lies a healthier third option: **move it to a queue**.

The shorter your queueing message templates, the better.

- “Received. I’ll reply during my 16:30 block.”
- “I have the details. I’ll organize and share them before 19:00 today.”
- “I’m in a work block right now, so I’ll handle this in 1 hour.”

That single sentence has three effects.

1. The other person feels less ignored.
2. I escape the pressure to make an immediate judgment.
3. A clear processing time reduces the residual load in my head.

Many people learn that “answering immediately” is a sign of diligence.  
But in an environment that demands high-quality execution, “clearly stating when you will answer” is often the more diligent habit.  
Input hygiene is not rudeness; it is designing predictability so work can be completed without delay.

## <span className="text-yellow-400 font-bold mb-6 mt-16 flex items-center gap-3">Apply It Today: A 15-Minute Input Hygiene Reset</span>

Spend just 15 minutes on this tonight.

1. Write down 5 input channels you check frequently.  
2. Label each channel Immediate / Today / Queue.  
3. Fix tomorrow’s check-in times in your calendar.  
4. Copy 2 queueing messages to the first lines of your notes app.  
5. Before starting a focus block, write down 3 items on your “do not check” list.

These five steps are not glamorous.  
But systems that protect a day rarely are.  
Repeatable rules that control small inputs last much longer than grand resolutions to change your life all at once.

The conclusion of CH12 is clear.

We lose attention before we lose time.  
And attention is a resource to manage through input hygiene, rather than one to defend through willpower.

The more we have to do, the more information we want to find.  
But that is precisely when we should first ask:

“Is this input something I need right now, or something that soothes my anxiety?”

Asking this just once each day changes the substance of that day.  
Perfect quiet may be impossible, but choosing to reduce unnecessary noise is always possible.  
Focus is not an ability born of silence; it is a skill that begins with boundaries.
