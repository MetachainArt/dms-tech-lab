Before reopening something you have studied, try writing what you remember of its explanation. This is retrieval practice: attempting to reconstruct knowledge from memory. Then compare your account with the source and correct missing conditions or misunderstandings. The everyday exercise proposed here draws on learning research; it is not a DMS training intervention whose results we have measured.

With a document open, one sentence supplies cues for the next. An unfamiliar term may make sense because the surrounding paragraph does part of the work. That experience can differ from explaining the material without it. A well-organized AI explanation can make the difference easy to miss: receiving a readable document may feel like finishing the learning. A brief recall attempt can separate those activities.

The original research papers were checked on October 17, 2026. They report experiments involving university students learning from texts. They do not establish the educational value of a particular AI product, long-term workplace performance, or a study duration that suits everyone.

## Can rereading sometimes work better?

Yes, repeated study performed better under an immediate-test condition in the research discussed here. Henry L. Roediger III and Jeffrey D. Karpicke's [2006 paper](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2006/2006_Roediger_Karpicke_PsychSci.pdf) compared repeated study of prose passages with recalling them without feedback. On a final test after five minutes, repeated study was more effective. On delayed tests after two days or one week, earlier retrieval practice produced better retention.

The timing matters. Difficulty remembering now does not establish that a learning session was worthless, and a passage that reads fluently again does not guarantee lasting recall. Repeated study increased students' confidence in their memory, but the delayed test results did not follow the same pattern as that confidence. The experience of learning and a later assessment can give different answers.

I would not use this finding to remove rereading. Unfamiliar material needs an initial reading, sometimes several careful ones. Closing a document will not repair an explanation you did not understand in the first place. My recommendation concerns the moment when you are about to reopen material you have already studied: first check what you can explain without seeing it.

## Does recalling a definition establish understanding?

Reciting a definition alone does not establish that you can apply it in a new situation. Jeffrey D. Karpicke and Janell R. Blunt's [2011 paper](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_Science.pdf) compared retrieval practice with making concept maps while viewing science texts. The advantage of retrieval practice was also observed on questions that required students to connect concepts and make inferences.

There is a useful procedural detail. In the retrieval condition, students recalled material, studied the text again, and recalled it again. Describing this as the result of closing a book and writing once would omit part of the activity. The comparison also does not establish that concept mapping is poor for every learning task. Read the conditions of the activity together with what the later test assessed.

For daily learning, I would make the question more specific than asking for a definition. After reading about an automation feature, explain what input it receives and what needs checking before its output is used. Add a condition under which changing the input would change the result. These are suggested questions for workplace learning, not reproductions of the researchers' tests.

Consider a hypothetical document-classification tool. You close the instructions and explain its categories, but cannot remember what should happen to a document that fits none of them. You can now reopen the source at the exception-handling section. If the source itself does not address that case, record the condition as unresolved rather than inventing an automatic behavior. This example is illustrative, not a customer incident or an experiment we ran.

## Is a blank answer useful?

A blank can identify where to return to the source, although persisting without a cue is not a goal in itself. If nothing comes to mind, try looking at a short heading or question before attempting the explanation again. Read the relevant section if that still does not help. Keep an answer written after seeing a cue separate from one produced without assistance.

I would choose one topic at a time. Reconstructing an entire article can turn a small check into a documentation task. Pick one judgment you expect to use, write an explanation without the source, and keep it beside the correction made after checking. The purpose is to locate an omitted condition, not to award points for a long performance from memory. Neither the number of sentences nor the time spent here is a validated optimum.

The following distinctions are proposed for this exercise. This is not a research-validated scoring instrument or an authorization table for workplace decisions.

| What the note contains | How to read it | What to do next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An explanation written without the source | What you retrieved unaided | Compare its conditions with the source |
| An explanation written after seeing a heading | What you retrieved with a cue | Mark where assistance was used |
| A correction made with the source open | What you checked and repaired | Preserve the first account beside it |
| A part you still cannot explain | A scope for further study or a question | Recheck approved source material |

Different wording does not automatically make an answer wrong. If your own explanation preserves the conditions and causal relationships, there is no need to replace it with the source's exact phrasing. An answer full of the correct terminology can still be wrong if it changes who or what a rule applies to. For restricted documents, follow the organization's storage requirements even when the notes are for personal learning.

## Should you ask AI for the answer first?

The order depends on what you are checking. If you want to find out what you can retrieve, read the AI answer afterwards. If you need a new explanation to understand the topic initially, use that assistance first. Just do not record both activities as unaided retrieval. Reading with support and explaining without support have different roles in this exercise.

AI-generated questions may be useful, but check that the questions and their proposed answers match the source. A question can introduce a condition that the material never supplied, or an answer can assert something absent from it. Having the same system grade its answer as correct does not finish source verification. Find the supporting passage in approved material and use the relevant professional review procedure for consequential technical judgments.

DMS's [AI training task boundary map](/en/works/ai-training-task-boundary-map) is a practical guide to deciding which tasks to delegate. One learning question is whether someone who has studied those boundaries can explain them without the guide. For example, explain why permitting a draft does not necessarily permit an external send. This extends a published DMS guide with a proposed study question; it is not a report of classroom outcomes.

[My Judgment Before the AI Answer](/en/blog/homo-technicus-judgment-before-ai) concerns recording reasons for accepting an answer. This essay concerns reconstructing material after studying it. The two checks examine different things: the grounds for a decision and the learner's ability to explain what was learned. Accurate recall does not make a source true, so source verification remains a separate task.

Keeping only the corrected response can make material look as though you knew it from the beginning. Leave the first account in place and put the repair below it. Omitting a term differs from reversing the condition under which a rule applies. I would add a short reason for the correction rather than give both the same undifferentiated wrong-answer mark.

There is no requirement to settle every gap that day. Leave background unrelated to the current task on a reading list, and verify the condition you need now. Perfect reconstruction is an awkward stopping rule for an everyday exercise. I would stop after checking the explanation needed for the task and marking the remaining uncertainty. If the source changes between attempts, note its title or check date so that a changed answer is not mistaken for a change in memory.

## Would you try the explanation again tomorrow?

I recommend answering the same question again after some time has passed. Immediately after correcting an answer, the source's wording may still be available. When you next need that knowledge, cover the earlier response and explain it again to find out what needs another reading. Tomorrow is a modest starting suggestion, not a claim about the best review interval for every kind of knowledge.

If maintaining a record becomes burdensome, keep a corrected sentence and the location of its source. Choose explanations you expect to reuse instead of turning every article into a test. For medical, legal, or safety procedures, verify figures and sequences against current official documentation even after a successful recall attempt. A memory exercise does not replace an operating manual.
