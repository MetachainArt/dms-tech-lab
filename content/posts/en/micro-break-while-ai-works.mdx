The time you spend waiting for an AI to answer can serve as a short break from work, and that may help with fatigue. In a 2022 meta-analysis of breaks of 10 minutes or less (22 samples, 2,335 people), breaks raised vigor and lowered fatigue. A gain in performance was not statistically confirmed. The study did not look at waiting for an AI, so the ways of using wait time in this article are proposals DMS.Labs built from the research, not results we measured.

Based on public sources checked on November 18, 2026. I read the full open-access text of "'Give me a break!' A systematic review and meta-analysis on the efficacy of micro-breaks for increasing well-being and performance" by Albulescu and five co-authors (PLOS ONE 17(8), e0272460, published August 31, 2022). The figures and tables are published as images, so I used only the numbers stated in the text, and I did not read the supporting tables or the supporting file describing each study. The paper does not study waiting for AI answers.

## What counts as a micro-break?

A pause of 10 minutes or less during work. The authors set the 10-minute ceiling on the basis of earlier work that calls the first minutes after effort "micro-recovery," and on a distinction that the next category runs from about 10 minutes to an hour. The breaks in the included studies ran from 8 seconds to 10 minutes.

What people did during the break varied too. Five studies used a mentally engaging activity such as watching a short clip, six used physical movement, six used relaxation, and five combined two activities. Of the 22 samples, 19 involved breaks unrelated to work, one involved a work-related break, and two fell into a combined category. That mix matters later, when we get to waiting for an AI.

The participants were healthy employees and students. Eleven samples were students, ten were employees, and one was described only as "normal volunteers." The mean age was 31.2. Thirteen samples were tested in a lab and nine in a workplace.

## Did breaks improve vigor and performance?

Vigor and fatigue improved. Performance did not clearly change. The standardized effect size (d) for vigor was 0.36 (9 samples, 913 people), higher for those who rested, and for fatigue it was 0.35 (9 samples, 803 people), lower for those who rested. Both were statistically significant. The d value is the difference between the two groups divided by the standard deviation, and 0.2 is usually read as small and 0.5 as medium, so these are small effects.

For performance, d was 0.16 (15 samples, 1,132 people) and not statistically significant. The abstract gives p = .116 and the results section gives p = .17. Either way it is above the .05 threshold.

Results on fatigue barely differed between studies, and results on vigor were fairly consistent. The authors found no evidence that break length, the preceding task, the type of participant, the setting, or the control condition changed the effect. Both outcomes were self-reported questionnaire scores, which is worth keeping in mind.

The authors add one more point. Among the papers they reviewed, none found that adding a break significantly lowered performance, even though the time on task was shorter.

## When did breaks help performance?

It depended on the kind of work done before the break. When the preceding task was a demanding cognitive one, d was -0.09 (p = .541), so there was no effect. For creative tasks it was 0.38 (p = .006), small but significant. For clerical tasks it was 0.56 (p = .047), based on only two studies, which the authors say to read with caution.

One possible reason the authors give is that a break of under 10 minutes can refill vigor without fully restoring the resources a demanding cognitive task needs. The abstract says recovering from highly depleting work may take more than a 10-minute break. They also note that the cognitive-task results stayed heterogeneous and that performance was measured in very different ways across studies, so those findings deserve extra care.

The vigor effect after cognitive tasks was also small and not significant. The differences between task types were not themselves statistically significant, so it is hard to say that the vigor effect differs by task.

## How long should a break be?

For performance, longer was better. For vigor and fatigue, length made no visible difference. Break length was the only significant moderator, and only for performance (b = 0.07, p = .006). The authors read the results as showing that breaks close to ten minutes reduce fatigue, raise vigor, and raise perceived performance, yet they also admit there is no agreed standard for the right length.

The paper does not say which activity is best. The authors report in their limitations that there were too few samples to compare break activities. For vigor and fatigue alone, they judge that any activity that lets you step away from work seems to be enough. That is their interpretation, not a comparison the data showed directly.

The introduction cites earlier studies in which work-related break activities were linked to lower well-being and poorer sleep. This meta-analysis had only one work-related break, so it could not compare directly, and that passage should be read as background only.

## How does this apply while waiting for an AI?

The study confirmed the effect of short breaks unrelated to work. How long an AI tool takes to answer varies by tool and task, and I did not measure it. The three points below are my interpretation, built by joining the results together.

First, opening another work window while you wait is probably a different task, not a break. The study could not analyze work-related breaks, so there is no basis for saying that time refills your energy.

Second, checking the answer you receive may be close to a demanding cognitive task. If so, the study's cognitive-task result suggests you should not expect a break to raise your checking accuracy. What you can hope for is less accumulated fatigue.

Third, when the wait is long, as when an agent works for several minutes, the finding that longer breaks gave larger performance effects is a useful reference. This relationship came from a comparison across 22 samples and was not tested under conditions like waiting for an AI.

The table below is an example this article proposes. It is not a method the research verified, and it is a starting point to adapt to the wait times in your own work.

| Wait time | How to rest | What the research confirmed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tens of seconds | Look away from the screen at the window or something far away | The shortest break was 8 seconds, but results were not split by length |
| 1 to 3 minutes | Stand up, stretch, or drink some water | Six studies used physical breaks, but activities were not compared |
| About 10 minutes | Step away and walk, then read the answer from the top when you return | The study's ceiling was 10 minutes, and the performance effect grew with length |
| Any length | Do not open other work email or messaging | Only one work-related break was included, so its effect could not be analyzed |

Here is a hypothetical scene. It is not a real case, only an assumption for illustration. A staff member who runs the administration of a small tutoring school hands an AI a draft of next month's timetable notice. If she opens a tuition email while she waits, her mind is already elsewhere when the draft arrives. Had she decided in advance to stand up, drink a glass of water, and come back, she would read the draft for the first time at her desk. What changes is where she was when she opened it. It does not mean the draft gets better.

## How far can this research be trusted?

There are several cautions in how it should be read.

The study quality was not high. Of the 22 samples, only 4 were rated low risk of bias, and 13 did not state their randomization method. Vigor and fatigue were measured only by self-report. There were also only 9 samples each for vigor and fatigue.

Some values were excluded. One fatigue study with d = 13.43 and one performance study with d = 3.85 were treated as outliers and removed. The authors explain that the differences between studies shrank greatly after removal.

The performance result may reflect publication bias. Egger's test was significant (p = .048), and the bias-adjusted value was 0.22, still a small effect. The authors wrote that the bias does not appear to be a serious threat to the results.

The search was also limited. It covered only English-language papers from January 1990 to April 2021 in SCOPUS, MEDLINE, and PsycINFO, and it does not reflect how people have worked since the pandemic. Half the samples were students, so applying the findings to employees calls for care.

Pick the one moment when you most often wait for an AI and decide how you will rest then. Take one row from the table above and put it in the words of your own work. After a week, look back only at how many times you took the break and how you read the answer when you returned. I should also be clear about what this article did not confirm. The research does not include waiting for an AI answer, so its effect sizes cannot be carried over directly. I found no evidence that a break improves the accuracy of checking an AI answer.
