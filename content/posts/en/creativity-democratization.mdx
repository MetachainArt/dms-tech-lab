<div className="lead text-2xl text-white font-sans font-medium mb-12 leading-relaxed">
    In the summer of 2022, the release of Midjourney and Stable Diffusion was like a Gutenberg revolution for the art world. Visual expression, once attainable only through decades of apprenticeship and painful training, was replaced by a few lines in a prompt box. It was a clear liberation. Novelists unable to draw could visualize their worlds, and indie game developers without capital could put AAA-level illustrations into games. The democratization of creativity had arrived. Yet behind the celebration lurks an unsettling shadow warning of the end of art.
</div>

## <span className="text-transparent bg-clip-text bg-gradient-to-r from-green-400 to-emerald-500">1. The Complete Disappearance of Aura: The End of Scarcity</span>

In 1936, observing photography and film, Walter Benjamin predicted the collapse of an artwork’s unique presence—its aura. AI completes that collapse. An illustration that takes a human a week is produced by AI in four versions within 3 seconds. As supply becomes infinite, an image’s value approaches zero.

Craftsmanship is no longer an essential artistic virtue. Elaborate brushwork and realistic depiction once inspired wonder; now they are abilities available to anyone. Where, then, does artistic value move? As when Marcel Duchamp placed a urinal before the public and called it Fountain, the important question becomes why rather than how. What image should be made? Why make it? We are being forced into an age of conceptual art in which the artist’s philosophy, narrative and curatorial ability matter more than brushstrokes.

## <span className="text-transparent bg-clip-text bg-gradient-to-r from-emerald-400 to-teal-500">2. Data Vampires and the Copyright Dilemma</span>

The astonishing performance of AI models is not a free lunch. It was made by drawing, without permission, on billions of publicly available internet images—the blood and sweat of countless creators. Ask it to imitate a living artist by saying "draw this in OOO’s style," and AI produces forgeries even the original artist may struggle to distinguish. Is this learning, or theft?

Law cannot keep pace with technology. The "No AI Art" movement is artists’ instinctive resistance for survival. Yet, just as the historical Luddite movement could not stop the Industrial Revolution, this trend may be impossible to reverse. In the future, Human Made Certification might be attached like an organic-food label. An ironic age may arrive in which human trembling brushstrokes and mistakes command high prices as evidence of authenticity, rather than AI’s smooth perfection.

## <span className="text-transparent bg-clip-text bg-gradient-to-r from-teal-400 to-cyan-500">3. The Hybrid Creator: Homo Prompticus</span>

There is more than pessimism. When cameras appeared, portrait painters lost jobs, but painting cast off the burden of reproducing its subjects and evolved into Impressionism and abstraction. A new kind of artist, the photographer, also emerged. AI will likewise create new artistic genres.

Future artists will resemble orchestra conductors more than artisans with brushes. They direct the vast orchestra of AI with a baton called the prompt, selecting the best generated results, retouching and generating again to complete the work. We might call this synthography or prompt-engineering art. These hybrid creators, using algorithms instead of brushes and data instead of paint, will lead a renaissance. Art does not die. Its tools change, and its definition expands.
